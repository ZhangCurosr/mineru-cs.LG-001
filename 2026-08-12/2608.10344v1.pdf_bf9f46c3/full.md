## Abstract

Thermo-mechanical compliant devices are commonly designed with small-strain linear elasticity and temperature-independent material properties, even though they might operate hundreds of kelvin above ambient where both assumptions are questionable. In this work, we quantify the efect and cost of each assumption in multi-material topology optimization of ther mally actuated compliant devices. To this end, we introduce a physics-informed, simultaneous analysis-and-design framework with (i) a finite-strain quadratic-Hencky (logarithmic-strain) constitutive model whose isotropic thermal eigenstrain admits an exact additive split in log-strain space, and (ii) temperature-dependent conductivity, thermal expansion, and elastic moduli for a titanium–copper–steel material system. We optimize a thermal actuator and a thermal gripper at three design temperatures under both a baseline model and the full physics, subject to mass and manufacturability constraints. Every converged design is re-evaluated by verified nonlinear finite element solvers in the full factorial of constitutive law and property model. The comparison between the two factors reveals that the constitutive law is the decisive modeling choice: These devices work as linkages where linear kinematics mistakes rotation for compressive strain; its error therefore grows with the design temperature and concentrates on the very layouts that exploit rotation best. Because a linear optimizer also steers away from the rotation-rich mechanisms that would expose this bias, the model can deceptively appear trustworthy when validated against its own designs. Designing with the full physics yields consistently stronger and more temperature-robust devices at a modest increase in design-time cost.<sup>1</sup>

Keywords: Topology Optimization, Thermo-Mechanical Design, Geometric Nonlinearity, Temperature-Dependent Properties, Compliant Mechanisms

## 1 Introduction

Topology optimization (TO) is an established computational design method that finds the optimal spatial distribution of material in a design domain directly from the governing physics and design requirements. TO has matured into a standard tool for designing compliant mechanisms, thermal devices, and multi-physics systems [1–5]. In thermo-mechanical TO, actuation is generated by constrained thermal expansion rather than external loads, a principle widely exploited in micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS) where electrothermal actuators and grippers convert Joule heating or a prescribed thermal environment into controlled strokes [6–9]. The design space is considerably richer in multi-material settings, where the optimizer arranges several candidate metals to exploit their unique material properties subject to mass and manufacturability constraints [10, 11].

Nearly all of this literature, including our own prior work [11], rests on two modeling conveniences. First, the mechanical response is described by small-strain linear elasticity, even though the slender members and distributed hinges that make a compliant mechanism efective undergo moderate rotations well before strains become large [12–14]. Second, material properties are held constant. In the thermal-actuator literature the constant values are often nondimensionalized and independent of the operating temperature [6–8,15]. Investigations that do use physical properties, adopt room temperature values despite the mismatch with operating temperatures [16,17]. Deng and Suresh [18], for example, state that their reference room temperature serves “only” to select material properties, and then optimize a titanium-alloy wing rib at ∆T = 270 K; our own prior work [11] likewise uses room-temperature moduli and conductivities at a 673 K boundary temperature. The convention is particularly damaging: at 673 K, copper (the dominant phase in the optimized devices of this work) has an elastic modulus 15% below its room-temperature value and a mean coeficient of thermal expansion 9% above it, while across the candidate metals the moduli drop by 15–21% and the thermal conductivity of steel falls by 26% [19–22]. A room-temperature-anchored model misrepresents not only the stifness of the device but also the thermal load that drives it.

Although restricted to linear elasticity, a small body of work does use temperature-dependent properties and reports concrete gains. Tang et al. [23] make conductivity, elastic tensor, and expansion coeficient temperature-dependent under large gradients, carrying the dependence through the adjoint, and find constant-property optimization significantly inaccurate; Zheng et al. [24] reach the same conclusion over 293– 773 K. For multi-material design, Chen et al. [25] report that the temperature-dependent modulus dominates compliance while the expansion coeficient principally reshapes the material distribution compared against room-temperature-anchored baselines. A better constant-property practice evaluates the properties at the design temperature; we therefore adopt this stronger convention as the baseline of this study and quantify the potential benefit of incorporating fully temperature-dependent properties. Both modeling conveniences bias the very objective that the optimizer maximizes, i.e., the predicted output stroke. Since the optimizer is free to exploit any inaccuracy of its physics model, such biases do not merely misreport performance; they can also steer the search toward designs whose advantage is an artifact of the model. Quantifying these efects, separately and jointly, is the main goal of this paper.

Geometrically nonlinear TO of compliant mechanisms has an established history built on Green–Lagrange kinematics with St. Venant–Kirchhof or neo-Hookean energies [12–14], together with interpolation schemes that stabilize low-density regions at finite strain [26]. Extensions to thermally driven finite-strain problems are comparatively recent: Chung et al. [27] combined level-set TO with nonlinear thermoelasticity, Sui et al. [28] designed thermo-hyperelastic structures via inverse-motion form finding, and Granlund et al. [29] optimized compliant mechanisms under transient thermal loading with a multi-material neo-Hookean model. Temperature-dependent properties have likewise received attention in thermo-elastic TO under large temperature gradients [23]. The Hencky (logarithmic) strain measure, however, has essentially not been used in thermo-mechanical TO; its appearances in TO are limited to purely mechanical settings such as hyperelastic ground structures [30] and material-point-method TO of large-deformation mechanisms [31]. This measure is particularly well suited to thermo-mechanical design: for isotropic thermal expansion, the logarithmic thermal eigenstrain acts as an exact additive and isotropic ofset to the elastic strain across arbitrary deformations, unlike the traditional Green–Lagrange eigenstrain, which is only first-order accurate with respect to thermal stretch. The quadratic-Hencky energy is furthermore an excellent model of real metals up to moderate elastic strains [32–34], precisely the regime of metallic compliant devices.

The majority of TO frameworks for thermomechanical design are formulated with classic density-based (SIMP) [1, 3] or level-set [8] methods and are implemented in a nested analysis and design fashion: every design update requires solving the governing PDEs to convergence, sensitivities follow from a separately derived adjoint problem and the design advances through its own update loop [2,5]. More recently, machine learning (ML) techniques have emerged as promising tools for TO. Early eforts focused primarily on datadriven surrogates or generative models trained on datasets produced by conventional TO solvers [35, 36]. Although these approaches can reduce computational cost, they remain tied to the physics represented in their training data, which makes them ill-suited to interrogating the physics. Physics-informed ML (PIML) ofers a more flexible paradigm in which the governing equations and the design objective are embedded directly into the learning process [37,38]. PIML provides mesh-free representations of the state and design fields that stay consistent with the underlying physics, accommodates additional design constraints as auxiliary loss terms and is diferentiable end to end. Consequently, the constitutive model reduces to an energy density in the loss, so upgrading the physics means modifying one term rather than a solver overhaul.

Building on this line of work, we have developed simultaneous, mesh-free TO frameworks based on physics-informed Gaussian processes (PIGPs) whose mean functions are parameterized with specialized neural networks [38–41] and extended them to multi-material and multi-physics problems [11]. All of these adopt linear kinematics and constant properties, and none enforces manufacturability. The present paper closes both gaps at once: it upgrades and interrogates the physics, and it does so under the manufacturing constraints (i.e., a minimum feature size and the exclusion of metallurgically incompatible material interfaces) that favor realizable multi-material thermo-mechanical designs. The unique contributions of this work are as follows:

• Building an ML-based TO framework considering finite deformations, multi-material designs, and manufacturability constraints, see Fig. 1.

• Introducing per-phase temperature-dependent conductivity, expansion, and modulus for a {Ti, Cu, Steel} system, fit to published measurements over 293–1100 K [19–22,42,43], with the eigenstrain computed as the exact integral of the instantaneous coeficient and every property–temperature pathway carried into the adjoint.

• Designing a controlled comparison between the two physics models and quantifying both modeling errors with temperature-robustness and transferability analyses: Anchoring the constant-property baseline at the design temperature isolates the constitutive law as the decisive modeling choice.

• Enforcing manufacturability throughout: a Helmholtz-type filter [44] for minimum feature size and an interface-exclusion penalty preventing brittle Ti–Fe intermetallics [45] so that the physics comparison is conducted over manufacturable layouts; we refer to the resulting framework as m-PIGP.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the governing physics, the two constitutive models, the temperature-dependent property model, and the optimization framework. Section 3 describes the cross-evaluation protocol and reports the optimized designs and the comparative studies. Section 4 summarizes the findings and outlines future work.

## 2 Methodology

We consider multi-material thermo-mechanical TO in a two-dimensional domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ occupied by $n _ { m }$ candidate materials and void. The design is the vector field $\pmb { \rho } ( \pmb { x } ) = [ \rho _ { 0 } , \rho _ { 1 } , . . . , \rho _ { n _ { m } } ] ^ { \top }$ of local volume fractions with $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { i = 0 } ^ { n _ { m } } \rho _ { i } ( { \pmb x } ) = 1 } \end{array}$ , where $i = 0$ denotes void. Phase-dependent properties are interpolated with the power law

$$
\mathcal { P } ( \pmb { x } , T ) = \sum _ { i = 0 } ^ { n _ { m } } \mathcal { P } _ { i } ( T ) \rho _ { i } ^ { p } ( \pmb { x } ) ,\tag{1}
$$

where $\mathcal { P } \in \{ E , \kappa , q _ { v } \}$ collects the Young’s modulus, thermal conductivity, and volumetric heat sink, $p$ is the penalization exponent (continuated from 1 to 3), and the coeficient of thermal expansion is interpolated linearly $( p = 1 )$ to avoid biasing the eigenstrain of intermediate densities. We use a uniform Poisson’s ratio $\nu = 0 . 3 1$ . The phase values $\mathcal { P } _ { i } ( T )$ are temperature dependent (Sec. 2.3).

## 2.2 Constitutive Models: Linear Versus Quadratic Hencky

The two constitutive models compared in this work share the same plane-stress elastic coeficients $C _ { 1 } =$ $E / ( 1 - \nu ^ { 2 } )$ and $C _ { 2 } = E / [ 2 ( 1 + \nu ) ]$ and the same isotropic thermal eigenstrain $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } .$ , and difer only in kinematics.

Linear elasticity (small strain). The strain is $\begin{array} { r } { \varepsilon = \frac { 1 } { 2 } ( \mathbf { H } + \mathbf { H } ^ { \top } ) } \end{array}$ and the energy density is the standard quadratic form

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \psi _ { L } ( \mathbf { H } ) = \frac { 1 } { 2 } \left( \varepsilon - \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } \mathbf { I } \right) : \mathbb { C } : \left( \varepsilon - \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } \mathbf { I } \right) , } \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

with C the plane-stress stifness. This is the kinematic model used in most of the thermo-mechanical TO literature.

Quadratic Hencky (finite strain). The material logarithmic strain is $\varepsilon _ { H } = { \textstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } } \ln ( \mathbf { F } ^ { \top } \mathbf { F } )$ , and the energy is the same quadratic form evaluated on the log strain,

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \psi _ { H } ( \mathbf { H } ) = \frac { 1 } { 2 } \left( \varepsilon _ { H } - \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } \mathbf { I } \right) : \mathbb { C } : \left( \varepsilon _ { H } - \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } \mathbf { I } \right) . } \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

The quadratic-Hencky model is frame indiferent, captures finite rotations exactly, and reproduces measured metal response up to moderate elastic strains [33, 34]; it reduces to Eq. (5) as $\mathbf { H }  \mathbf { 0 }$ . Two features make it particularly attractive for thermo-mechanical design. First, for isotropic expansion the multiplicative thermal split $\mathbf { F } = \mathbf { F } _ { e } ( \vartheta \mathbf { I } )$ with thermal stretch $\begin{array} { r } { \vartheta = \exp \big ( \int _ { T _ { \infty } } ^ { T } \alpha \mathrm { d } \tau \big ) } \end{array}$ commutes, so

$$
\ln \mathbf { U } = \ln \mathbf { U } _ { e } + \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } \mathbf { I } , \qquad \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } = \int _ { T _ { \infty } } ^ { T } \alpha ( \tau ) \mathrm { d } \tau ,\tag{7}
$$

i.e., the elastic log strain is obtained from the total log strain by subtracting an additive isotropic eigenstrain exactly at finite deformation, the property that makes Eqs. (5) and (6) formally identical and isolates the strain measure as the only diference between the two models. Second, in two dimensions $\varepsilon _ { H }$ admits a closed form: writing $\mathbf { C } = \mathbf { F } ^ { \top } \mathbf { F }$ with invariants $I _ { 1 } = \operatorname { t r } \mathbf { C }$ and det C, we use

$$
\begin{array} { r } { \pmb { \varepsilon } _ { H } = \frac { 1 } { 4 } \ln ( \operatorname* { d e t } \mathbf { C } ) \mathbf { I } + \frac { \pmb { f } ( q ) } { I _ { 1 } } \Big ( \mathbf { C } - \frac { 1 } { 2 } I _ { 1 } \mathbf { I } \Big ) , } \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

where $q = \left[ ( C _ { 1 1 } - C _ { 2 2 } ) ^ { 2 } + 4 C _ { 1 2 } ^ { 2 } \right] / I _ { 1 } ^ { 2 }$ and $f ( q ) = \mathrm { a t a n h } ( \sqrt { q } ) / \sqrt { q }$ is smooth on [0, 1). This parameterization avoids eigendecomposition, remains finite and diferentiable at coalescent stretches $( \mathbf { C } \propto \mathbf { I } )$ and admits stable second derivatives, thereby removing the matrix-logarithm diferentiation cost that has discouraged Hencky-strain adjoints in TO [31].

Because void regions cannot be allowed to distort or invert at finite strain, our element energy uses the three-term interpolation of Wang et al. [26],

$$
\psi = \psi _ { H } ( \gamma \mathbf { H } ) + \psi _ { L } ( \mathbf { H } ) - \psi _ { L } ( \gamma \mathbf { H } ) ,\tag{9}
$$

where $\gamma ( \pmb { x } ) \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ is a sharp smooth-Heaviside indicator of the local solid fraction. Solid material $( \gamma \approx 1 )$ sees the full nonlinear energy while void $( \gamma \approx 0 )$ sees small-strain elasticity with the void stifness.

We emphasize that the popular two-term shortcut $\psi _ { H } ( \gamma \mathbf { H } ) + ( 1 - \gamma ^ { 2 } ) \psi _ { L } ( \mathbf { H } )$ relies on the homogeneity $\psi _ { L } ( \gamma \mathbf { H } ) = \gamma ^ { 2 } \psi _ { L } ( \mathbf { H } )$ , which fails in the presence of a thermal eigenstrain (the cross term is linear in strain); the three-term form of Eq. (9) is exact for any $\varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } }$ and cancels the spurious pure-eigenstrain energy at $\gamma = 0$ identically. Under the linear model, Eq. (9) collapses to $\psi _ { L } ( \mathbf { H } )$ and $\gamma$ is inert.

## 2.3 Temperature-Dependent Material Properties

Our candidate set is {void, Ti, Cu, Steel}, three metals that span a broad range of conductivity, expansion, and stifness, giving the optimizer genuine material contrast to exploit. All three materials remain solid over the design temperatures considered here: at the highest, $T _ { D } = 1 0 7 3$ K, copper is the most severely loaded at ${ \sim } 0 . 8$ of its melting temperature (1358 K), while steel (≈ 1800 K) and titanium (1941 K) sit near 0.6 and 0.55 of theirs.

Titanium stays in its α phase throughout, its transus lying at 1155 K, so a single set of property fits covers the whole window. At such homologous temperatures the metals would creep under sustained load, so the devices are understood to operate intermittently, in short actuation cycles as is typical of thermally driven MEMS. This is the regime in which the rate-independent elastic response of Eqs. (5) and (6) applies, and it is the operating mode assumed throughout this work.

## 2.4 Simultaneous Optimization Framework

The designs are produced with the m-PIGP framework, which augments our multi-material PIGP approach [11] with manufacturability constraints; we summarize it briefly below and extend it to the new physics, referring the reader to Appendix A for details on field parameterization as well as constraint and loss formulations.

As shown in Figure 1, the primal fields $( \pmb { u } , T )$ , the temperature adjoint $\mu ,$ and the design field $\rho$ are parameterized by independent Gaussian process (GP) priors whose mean functions are parametric grid convolutional attention networks (PGCANs) [41]; conditioning the GPs on boundary data enforces Dirichlet BCs exactly [38, 39], and a softmax head enforces the partition of unity on $\rho .$ . All parameters are trained simultaneously with Adam [46] on a loss that combines the (adjoint-augmented) design objective, the deepenergy-method residuals $( \Pi _ { u } , \Pi _ { T } $ , and $\Pi _ { \mu }$ of the primal and adjoint problems), and the design constraints; the full loss $\mathcal { L }$ is assembled in $\operatorname { E q . } \left( 2 2 \right)$ of Appendix A. The continuous fields are evaluated on a single uniform $2 0 0 \times 1 0 0$ grid of bilinear quadrilaterals with full $2 \times 2$ Gauss quadrature that hosts the energies, design field, sensitivities, and adjoint solve to eliminate inter-grid transfer operators.

The design sensitivities that drive the update of $\rho$ are obtained from an adjoint analysis that accounts for the finite-strain kinematics and for all property–temperature couplings. Since both aspects difer substantially from the small-strain, constant-property adjoint of our previous work, we derive them in Sec. 2.5.

We enforce manufacturability by two mechanisms that are detailed in Appendix $\mathrm { A } \colon \mathrm { a }$ Helmholtz PDE filter [44] with radius $r = 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ controls the minimum feature size and suppresses islands, and a pairwise interface-exclusion penalty prevents direct Ti–Steel contact, which forms brittle $\mathrm { T i - F e }$ intermetallics [45,47]. The mass constraint limits the design to 25% of the mass of a fully dense copper domain and is ramped over the first half of training together with the SIMP exponent $( p : 1  3 )$ ; the interface penalty follows its own continuation schedule (Appendix A).

We run each course of optimization for 10,000 epochs with five independent initializations. For each device, we set the design temperature to $T _ { D } \in \{ 6 7 3 , 8 7 3 , 1 0 7 3 \}$ K and specify the physics model (baseline:

linear elasticity with $T _ { D }$ -anchored constant properties; high-fidelity: Hencky kinematics with temperaturedependent properties). This procedure produces 60 optimized designs.

## 2.5 Adjoint Sensitivity Analysis

The stroke objective depends on the design only through the coupled state problem, which is nonlinear in both u (finite strain) and T (temperature-dependent conductivity). We let ${ \pmb R } _ { T } ( { \bf T } ; \pmb { \rho } ) = { \bf 0 }$ and $\begin{array} { r l } { R _ { u } ( \mathbf { u } ; \mathbf { T } , \rho ) = } & { { } } \end{array}$ ${ \partial \Pi _ { u } } / { \partial { \bf { u } } } = { \bf { 0 } }$ for the discrete thermal and mechanical residuals on the unified grid, with $\Pi _ { u }$ from Eq. (4). The coupling is one-way: heat transfer does not depend on u. We augment the objective as $\mathcal { I } = u _ { \mathrm { o u t } }$ $\lambda ^ { \top } R _ { u } - \mu ^ { \top } R _ { T }$ (distinct from the training loss $\mathcal { L }$ of Appendix A) and require stationarity with respect to both states. This defines the two adjoint problems below.

Displacement adjoint. Stationarity with respect to u gives

$$
{ \bf K } _ { T } ( { \bf u } ^ { * } ) \boldsymbol { \lambda } = e _ { \mathrm { o u t } } , \qquad { \bf K } _ { T } = \frac { \partial ^ { 2 } \Pi _ { u } } { \partial { \bf u } \partial { \bf u } } \bigg \vert _ { { \bf u } ^ { * } } .\tag{13}
$$

Here $e _ { \mathrm { o u t } }$ is a unit load at the output port in the output direction, and ${ \bf K } _ { T }$ is the consistent tangent at the converged state. It contains the material part, the geometric (initial-stress) part, and the port-spring contribution $K _ { s }$ . Because the thermally pre-stressed members carry compression, ${ \bf K } _ { T }$ is symmetric but can be indefinite; we therefore solve Eq. (13) with a sparse $\mathrm { L D L ^ { T } }$ factorization [48] rather than energy minimization.

We assemble ${ \bf K } _ { T }$ from batched per-element Hessians of the interpolated energy in Eq. (9), using vectorized automatic diferentiation. This is exact for the three-term form, and the parameterization of Eq. (8) keeps the second derivatives bounded even at coalescent stretches. We then impose $\mathbf { \nabla } \lambda = \mathbf { 0 }$ on exactly the kinematically constrained degrees of freedom of the primal problem. During training, we refresh the factorization every 25 epochs after a 2000-epoch primal warm-up and treat the adjoint field as constant (detached) between refreshes.

Temperature adjoint. Stationarity with respect to T gives $( \partial { \mathbf { } } R _ { T } / \partial { \mathbf { T } } ) ^ { \top } \pmb { \mu } = - ( \partial { \mathbf { } } R _ { u } / \partial { \mathbf { T } } ) ^ { \top } \pmb { \lambda }$ . Since κ depends on T, the thermal tangent is nonsymmetric and its transpose yields the weak problem: find $\pmb { \mu }$ with $\mu = 0$ on the hot edge such that for all admissible $w .$

$$
\begin{array} { r l } {  { \int _ { \Omega } \kappa \boldsymbol { \nabla } w \cdot \boldsymbol { \nabla } \mu \mathrm { d } V + \int _ { \Omega } \frac { \partial \kappa } { \partial T } \Bigr \rvert _ { T ^ { * } } \bigl ( \nabla T ^ { * } \cdot \boldsymbol { \nabla } \mu \bigr ) w \mathrm { d } V } } \\ & { = \int _ { \Omega } \Bigl [ \alpha _ { \mathrm { i n s t } } \mathcal { T } _ { \lambda } - \frac { \partial _ { T } E } { E } \mathcal { S } _ { \lambda } \Bigr ] w \mathrm { d } V , } \end{array}\tag{14}
$$

where $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i n s t } } = \partial \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } / \partial T$ is the instantaneous expansion coeficient of Eq. (10).

We emphasize that the second term on the left never appears in the primal solve. κ is frozen at the previous temperature guess, so each Picard step is a linear conduction problem and never diferentiates κ with respect to T. The adjoint, however, linearizes the converged nonlinear residual ${ \pmb R } _ { T } ( { \bf T } ) = { \bf 0 }$ itself, which does depend on T through $\kappa ( T )$ , so omitting this term would transpose the Picard operator instead of the true tangent and bias every sensitivity.

The right-hand side collects the two paths through which the mechanical residual senses temperature: an eigenstrain path, from thermal expansion generating internal force, and a modulus path, from $E ( T )$ softening the stifness directly. For solid material, the eigenstrain path is

$$
\mathcal { T } _ { \lambda } = \frac { E } { 1 - \nu } \mathbf { F } ^ { - \top } : \nabla \lambda ,\tag{15}
$$

which follows from the identity tr $\varepsilon _ { H } =$ ln det F, whose directional derivative along λ is exactly $\mathbf { F } ^ { - \top }$ ∇λ. This is the only change finite strain makes to the classical small-strain trace term tr ∇λ. Under the interpolation of Eq. (9), the two branches combine as $\gamma \mathbf { F } _ { \gamma } ^ { - \top } : \nabla \lambda + ( 1 - \gamma ) \operatorname { t r } \nabla \lambda ;$ the linear branch of the three-term form carries weight $1 - \gamma$ because the eigenstrain cross term is linear in the strain.

The modulus path exploits a diferent structure: every term of the interpolated energy is linear-homogeneous in the local modulus $E ( \rho , T )$ . The energy’s temperature derivative is therefore its own directional derivative along the adjoint, $\mathcal { S } _ { \lambda } = \mathrm { d } \psi ( \mathbf { H } ^ { * } + \tau \nabla \lambda ) / \mathrm { d } \tau | _ { \tau = 0 }$ , scaled by $\partial _ { T } E / E$

For constant properties, $\partial _ { T } \kappa = \partial _ { T } E = 0$ and $\alpha _ { \mathrm { i n s t } } ~ = ~ \hat { \alpha } ( T _ { D } )$ , so Eq. (14) reduces to a self-adjoint conduction problem driven by the eigenstrain path alone. Linear kinematics then recovers the classical small-strain framework, with the source in its tr ∇λ form. Under Hencky kinematics, the source retains its interpolated finite-strain form even at constant properties.

We parametrize $\pmb { \mu }$ by its own GP-conditioned network and train it on the functional $\Pi _ { \mu }$ whose stationarity condition is Eq. (14), with the nonsymmetric term entering in the same Picard-lagged fashion as the primal properties, $\mathrm { i . e . , ~ } \partial _ { T } \kappa$ is evaluated at the previous temperature iterate, so at the fixed point the assembled operator is the true tangent.

Design sensitivity. With the adjoints so defined, the total derivative reduces to partial derivatives at frozen states,

$$
\frac { \mathrm { d } u _ { \mathrm { o u t } } } { \mathrm { d } \rho _ { i } } = - \frac { \partial W _ { \lambda } } { \partial \rho _ { i } } - \int _ { \Omega } \frac { \partial \kappa } { \partial \rho _ { i } } \nabla T ^ { * } \cdot \nabla \mu \mathrm { d } V + \int _ { \Omega } \frac { \partial q _ { v } } { \partial \rho _ { i } } \mu \mathrm { d } V ,\tag{16}
$$

where $W _ { \lambda } ( \pmb { \rho } ) : = \mathrm { d } \Pi _ { \mathrm { i n t } } ( \mathbf { H } ^ { * } + \tau \nabla \pmb { \lambda } ; \pmb { \rho } ) / \mathrm { d } \tau | _ { \tau = 0 }$ is the adjoint-weighted internal virtual work, evaluated with frozen states $( \mathbf { u } ^ { * } , \lambda , T ^ { * } )$ but design-live coeficients.

This definition fuses the three mechanical design pathways into one object: a single automatic-diferentiation pass through $W _ { \lambda }$ carries the stifness path $\partial \mathbb { C } / \partial \rho .$ the eigenstrain path $\partial \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } / \partial \rho _ { ; }$ and the interpolation-factor path $\partial \gamma / \partial \rho$ simultaneously.

Finally, the same automatic diferentiation pulls the gradient of Eq. (16) back through the Helmholtz filter and the softmax partition of unity (Appendix $\operatorname { A } ) ;$ ; since the filter map $( \mathbf { \dot { I } } - r ^ { 2 } \Delta ) ^ { - 1 }$ is self-adjoint, this is equivalent to filtering the sensitivity field itself.

## 3 Results and Discussion

Putting a modeling assumption on trial naturally raises two distinct questions: how much it distorts the predicted performance of a given design layout, and to what extent the design is improved when the optimizer utilizes the higher-fidelity physics model. This section answers them separately. Sections 3.1–3.3 establish the evaluation error at fixed design and trace it to its kinematic origin, Sec. 3.4 measures the design-time payof and what it costs to obtain, and Sec. 3.5 investigates how the resulting devices behave away from their design point.

Comparing designs by the objective values reported by their own optimizers would conflate model bias with design quality. We therefore freeze every converged design at its categorical material map (each element assigned to void, Ti, Cu, or Steel) and re-solve it with a standalone finite element evaluator on the same grid, weak forms, and BCs, under all four physics models

$$
\{ \mathrm { l i n e a r , H e n c k y } \} \times \{ \mathrm { c o n s t a n t ~ ( a t ~ } T _ { D } ) , T \mathrm { - d e p e n d e n t ~ p r o p e r t i e s } \} ,
$$

and at all three temperatures $T _ { D } \in \{ 6 7 3 , 8 7 3 , 1 0 7 3 \}$ K, regardless of the design’s training conditions. The thermal problem is solved by Picard iteration (tolerance $1 0 ^ { - 1 1 } )$ ; the linear-elastic problems by a single sparse direct solve; and the Hencky problems by incremental Newton–Raphson with the consistent tangent, ramping the thermal eigenstrain in four increments with automatic cutback. Incrementation is not cosmetic: at zero displacement under the full thermal load, the compressive thermal pre-stress renders the tangent indefinite, and Newton from the undeformed state diverges. Convergence requires the maximum residual to drop below $1 0 ^ { - 1 1 }$ of its initial value in double precision. The evaluator’s constitutive routines are verified against the training implementation at the source level, and against analytical solutions by free-thermalexpansion patch tests, for which the four models give four distinct exact answers $( \mathrm { e . g . , } u = ( e ^ { \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } } - 1 ) X$ for Hencky versus $u = \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } X$ for linear kinematics), reproduced to a relative error below $1 0 ^ { - 1 0 }$

For each design evaluated at its own $T _ { D }$ , we report the four strokes $u ( \mathcal { C } , \mathcal { P } )$ with constitutive law ${ \mathcal { C } } \in$ {lin, Hen} and property model $\mathcal { P } \in \ \{ \mathrm { c o n s t } , T \mathrm { - d e p } \}$ , and define, normalizing by the reference cell $u _ { \mathrm { o u t } } ^ { \mathrm { r e f } } \ =$ $u ( \mathrm { H e n } , T \mathrm { - d e p } )$ :

$$
\Delta _ { \mathrm { l a w } } ^ { \mathcal { P } } = \big [ u ( \mathrm { H e n } , \mathcal { P } ) - u ( \mathrm { l i n } , \mathcal { P } ) \big ] / u _ { \mathrm { o u t } } ^ { \mathrm { r e f } } ,\tag{17}
$$

$$
\Delta _ { \mathrm { p r o p } } ^ { \mathcal { C } } = \left[ u ( \mathcal { C } , T \mathrm { - d e p } ) - u ( \mathcal { C } , \mathrm { c o n s t } ) \right] / u _ { \mathrm { o u t } } ^ { \mathrm { r e f } } ,\tag{18}
$$

i.e., the constitutive-law efect at fixed property model and the property efect at fixed constitutive law; their diference across levels is the interaction $\Delta _ { \mathrm { l a w } } ^ { \mathrm { v } _ { T - \mathrm { d e p } } } - \Delta _ { \mathrm { l a w } } ^ { \mathrm { c o n s t } }$ . The self-assessment bias of a design family is the diference between the stroke in its native cell (the model it was optimized with) and the reference cell. Finally, evaluating every design under the reference physics at all three temperatures quantifies temperature robustness (stroke versus operating temperature) and transferability (which family, defined by design constitutive law and training $T _ { D }$ , performs best at each operating temperature).

## 3.1 Optimized Layouts and Material Allocation

The high-fidelity and baseline design families primarily difer in their geometric layouts rather than their overall material composition and mass. Hence, the performance discrepancies reported throughout this section stem almost entirely from how the design physics model distributes the material spatially. Figure 4 shows the median-performing design of each family, Fig. 5 instantiates how such a design emerges over a single optimization run, and Table 1 summarizes the reference-physics stroke and the realized mass of all sixty manufactured designs. Three observations frame the comparisons that follow.

## 3.2 Evaluation-Model Error: Constitutive Law Versus Property Model

A systematic cross-evaluation of the optimal designs demonstrates that the impact of the two physics modeling assumptions is markedly disproportionate. The material property model, whether the properties presumed are uniform or spatially varying, afects the performance negligibly compared to the choice of constitutive law. Figure 6 shows the mean stroke of each design family in the four combinations of assumptions at its own design temperature, and Fig. 7 condenses them into the factorial efects of Eqs. (17)–(18). We make three main observations. First, the constitutive-law efect is the dominant modeling error, and it is both strongly temperature- and design-dependent. Switching the evaluator from linear to Hencky kinematics raises the predicted stroke from 2–3% at 673 K to 8–11% at 1073 K on average (Fig. 7). The average metric, however, masks the nature of this error. The large spread at high temperature is not noise but systematic design-to-design variability: at 1073 K, the high-fidelity families are misjudged by an average of 16% (actuator) and 19% (gripper) of the family stroke, compared to about 1% for the baseline families. What the error tracks is the compound efect of rotation content and performance. Designs optimized with the Hencky model exploit finite-rotation kinematics that linear analysis cannot represent, and how badly a layout is misjudged correlates with how much rotation it carries. Because rotation and maximum stroke do not perfectly align within a family, extreme values like the 34% case in Fig. 8 represent outliers rather than the norm. Section 3.3 traces the kinematic roots of these inaccuracies.

Second, once the constant properties are anchored at the design temperature, the property model barely matters. In Fig. 6, the constant-property bar and the T-dependent bar of the same constitutive law are indistinguishable in every family and at every design temperature. The insets of Fig. 7 magnify the diference: the mean property efect never exceeds 0.31% of the reference stroke, and even the error-bar extremes stay below half a percent. The physical reason is that these conduction-driven devices feature a hot edge held at $T _ { D }$ and a mild distributed heat sink so the temperature falls no more than ∼ 60 K below $T _ { D }$ . Since the secant expansion coeficient is exact at $T _ { D }$ by construction, the properties frozen at the driving temperature track the solution of spatially varying T-dependent properties with suficient accuracy. Therefore, selecting an appropriate anchoring point for our designs rather than relying on the common room-temperature convention [11,18] justifies the assumption of uniform material properties across the domain.

## 3.3 Kinematic Origin of the Constitutive-Law Error

These thermal devices deform as linkages: nearly rigid limbs swinging about compliant hinges, through angles large enough that the small-strain measure charges the rotation itself as a spurious compressive strain comparable to the thermal eigenstrain that drives the device. This error accounts for the whole pattern of Sec. 3.2: why $\Delta _ { \mathrm { l a w } }$ grows with temperature, and why it falls hardest on the layouts that rotate the most. Under the reference physics, the displacement gradients in the solid grow almost linearly with the design temperature $\left( \mathrm { F i g . \ 8 ( a ) } \right)$ , while the elastic logarithmic strains stay at the percent level away from local hinge concentrations: the deformation is rotation dominated. Figures $^ { 8 ( \mathrm { b , c ) } }$ therefore color code the deformed configurations of the most severely misjudged design by the local rigid-rotation angle θ from the polar decomposition $\mathbf { F } = \mathbf { R } \mathbf { U }$ . Each limb rotates almost rigidly by up to ${ \sim } 8 ^ { \circ }$ and θ changes abruptly across the compliant hinges that connect the limbs to the frame and accommodate rotation.

This is where linear kinematics fails. The small-strain measure assigns strain energy to the symmetric part of the displacement gradient, a bookkeeping that is exact only to first order in the rotation: a pure rotation by θ carries the spurious compressive normal strain cos $\theta - 1 \approx - \theta ^ { 2 } / 2$ , which at the observed hinge rotations is comparable to the thermal eigenstrain $\left( \varepsilon _ { \mathrm { t h } } \approx 1 . 5 \% \right)$ that drives the entire device, whereas the logarithmic measure correctly assigns it zero strain. Under the linear model the hinges and the limbs they carry are thus artificially stifened as seen in Fig. 8(b) with visibly smaller hinge rotations and part of the thermal expansion is absorbed as spurious elastic strain instead of being released into output stroke. Since the Hencky-designed layouts deliberately develop such rotating members (Sec. 3.1), the linear evaluator penalizes them significantly.

## 3.4 Design Reference Physics Payof

Sections 3.2 and 3.3 asked how accurately a fixed design is evaluated; this section asks whether the better physics also finds better designs. Since a practitioner ultimately carries forward the single best design a TO routine produces, we compare the best seed of each family, with both selection and evaluation performed by one common judge. Figure 9 reports the outcome under all four judges. The verdict of the reference judge (boxed row) is clear: the Hencky-designed layout wins all six device–temperature combinations, by 4–12% of stroke. This margin is pure design-time payof: both candidates are judged by the same physics, so none of the evaluation bias of Sec. 3.2 enters it.

The rows above the reference show what happens when the judge itself is biased. At 673 and 873 K, the gripper’s advantage is large enough to survive even a linear referee. At 1073 K it is not: both linear judges rank the baseline design first, on both devices, so a practitioner who screens candidates with a linear tool discards the better design precisely where the payof is largest. The self-assessment numbers sharpen this warning. Because the baseline families misjudge their own optimal designs by only about one percent, validating a linear model against its own results creates a false sense of reliability. It appears trustworthy precisely because it avoids the rotation-rich layouts that would expose its fundamental limitations (Sec. 3.3).

The payof is also cheap to obtain. On identical single-GPU hardware (NVIDIA RTX 4090), a complete 10,000-epoch optimization takes $5 5 4 \pm 7$ s for the actuator and $6 4 7 \pm 1 7$ s for the gripper under the baseline physics, versus $7 8 8 \pm 1 1$ s and $8 7 2 \pm 1 4$ s under the full physics (mean ± one standard deviation over the five seeds). These cost factors of 1.42 and 1.35 are essentially independent of $T _ { D }$ . This modest overhead arises because the simultaneous formulation never re-solves the state problem to convergence within a design step: the finite-strain model adds the three-term energies of Eq. (9) and periodic tangent factorizations for the direct adjoint, bypassing a nested Newton loop around every analysis. Thus, critical design-time fidelity adds about forty percent computational cost.

A final observation concerns the collapsed seeds visible in Fig. 6 and in the min columns of Table 1. These weak designs are not an artifact of finite-strain physics. They are seed-correlated across families: the same initializations collapse for the baseline and the high-fidelity frameworks alike on the same devices and design temperatures. This indicates that the rough optimization landscape is intrinsic to the simultaneous thermo-mechanical TO problem itself and worsens at higher temperatures. Thus, multi-start optimization is essential for both families.

## 3.5 Temperature Robustness and Transferability

A device rarely operates at exactly its intended design temperature. To assess the robustness of the optimal designs obtained at a specific $T _ { D }$ against of-design thermal conditions, we re-evaluate the (best) designs on driving temperatures not seen during training using reference physics (i.e., Hencky constitutive law) and report the output stroke in Fig. 10. This analysis reveals two key findings.

First, every best design transfers gracefully to of-design temperatures: the stroke is nearly afine in the operating temperature, with mid-range curvatures one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the end-to-end stroke spans. The rankings among the best designs are consequently almost independent of the operating temperature, so a design need not be operated where it was trained.

Second, the ranking is topped by Hencky-designed layouts in all six device–temperature combinations and, notably, not by those trained at the highest temperature. The Hencky design trained at 873 K is the best, or within a fraction of a percent of the best in every combination; for the gripper it outperforms even the 1073 K-trained Hencky design at 1073 K itself. The best-design view shows that this is a statement about attainable optima rather than about averages: the deficit of the gripper family trained at 1073 K is not caused by its weaker seeds, since even the best of its five runs falls short of the best 873 K-trained design at its own operating temperature. The rougher high-temperature training landscape thus degrades the optima themselves. The practical guideline that follows is to optimize with the full physics at a moderate design temperature and rely on the near-afine temperature response, rather than re-optimizing at every operating point with a less reliable high-temperature run.

## 4 Conclusion

This paper examines two standard simplifying assumptions in thermo-mechanical topology optimization: small-strain kinematics and constant material properties. We develop a simultaneous physics-informed framework to include temperature-dependent, thus spatially varying material properties for a {Ti, Cu, Steel} system and a plane-stress quadratic-Hencky constitutive model, where thermal eigenstrain acts as an exact additive ofset in logarithmic strain space. We account for manufacturability constraints by discouraging the Ti–Steel interface and applying a Helmholtz-type filter to the density field. We then re-evaluated all sixty converged designs using verified finite element solvers across a full factorial of constitutive laws and property models (720 solves in total). To ensure a fair comparison, the constant-property baseline was anchored at the design temperature $T _ { D }$

The results reveal a sharp asymmetry between the two assumptions. These devices function as linkages with stif limbs pivoting about compliant hinges, and linear kinematics charges the rotation itself as a spurious compressive strain comparable to the thermal eigenstrain that drives the device: its error grows from 2–3% of stroke at 673 K to 8–11% at 1073 K. For the layouts that rely most heavily on rotation, this error consumes up to a third of the stroke and inverts the ranking of the best designs at high temperatures. In contrast, the constant-property model that is anchored at the design temperature alters the results by less than one percent. However, we note that the constant-property assumption would induce higher errors if anchored at a less physically relevant temperature, or if the devices were subjected to temperature gradients significantly larger than those present in this study.

The practical guidance is correspondingly simple. We anchor constant properties at the design temperature and adopt finite-strain kinematics whenever the mechanism relies on rotation: in the simultaneous framework the upgrade replaces one energy density in the training loss rather than a solver; it costs a factor of about 1.4 in design time, returns 4–12% more best-design stroke and produces the most temperature-robust devices with moderate-temperature designs transferring best along the nearly afine stroke–temperature response. Lastly, since a linear model misjudges its own designs by only about one percent while misjudging the best designs by far more, design tools should be audited by independent higher-fidelity re-evaluation rather than by self-assessment.

The extensions we consider most pressing address the physics this study deliberately held fixed. First, material nonlinearity: at high homologous temperatures (copper at 1073 K operates near 0.8 of its melting point) metals yield, creep and relax, and the quadratic-Hencky energy is precisely the elastic core of multiplicative finite-strain inelasticity so the framework extends naturally to plasticity- and creep-aware design where we should consider stress and stability constraints. Second, transient response: replacing steady conduction with transient thermal loading and Joule-heating coupling would let the optimizer shape actuation bandwidth and cyclic behavior rather than a single hot state. This is particularly important for capturing the regime in which creep and thermal ratcheting accumulate over repeated strokes. Finally, optimizing over a range of operating temperatures, rather than at a single design point, would maximize expected or worst-case performance across an operating window and yield devices that tolerate uncertain and drifting thermal environments by construction. The transferability results of this study, where moderately hot training temperatures already produce the strongest all-around designs are an encouraging starting point. These steps would elevate the framework from a controlled model trial to robust design of dynamically actuated devices in service.

## A.1 GP Field Parameterization with Exact Boundary Conditions

The primal fields (u, T), the temperature adjoint µ, and the design field ρ are each assigned an independent GP prior. For the multi-output fields (e.g., the displacement vector and the $( n _ { m } \mathrm { + } 1 )$ )-phase design field) we follow [11] and pair independent kernels across outputs with a single shared multi-output mean function, which captures inter-output dependencies through the mean while keeping the conditioning of each output numerically robust and inexpensive. The displacement adjoint λ is deliberately not parameterized this way: it is a solved field, obtained from the sparse direct factorization of Eq. (13).

Taking the displacement field as an example, the field evaluated at an arbitrary query point $\pmb { x } ^ { * } \in \Omega$ is the GP posterior mean conditioned on the prescribed boundary data:

$$
u _ { i } ( \pmb { x } ^ { * } ; \pmb { \theta } _ { u } , \phi _ { u } ) = m _ { u _ { i } } ( \pmb { x } ^ { * } ; \pmb { \theta } _ { u } ) + \pmb { w } _ { u _ { i } } ^ { \top } \pmb { r } _ { u _ { i } } ,\tag{19a}
$$

$$
{ \pmb w } _ { u _ { i } } = { \pmb c } _ { u } ^ { - 1 } ( { \pmb x } _ { u _ { i } } , { \pmb x } _ { u _ { i } } ; \phi _ { u } ) { \pmb c } _ { u } ( { \pmb x } _ { u _ { i } } , { \pmb x } ^ { * } ; \phi _ { u } ) ,\tag{19b}
$$

$$
r _ { u _ { i } } = \tilde { u } _ { i } - m _ { u _ { i } } ( { \pmb x } _ { u _ { i } } ; \pmb \theta _ { u } ) , \qquad i = 1 , 2 ,\tag{19c}
$$

where $m _ { u _ { i } } ( \cdot ; \pmb { \theta } _ { u } )$ is the shared mean function (Sec. A.2), $\pmb { c } _ { u } ( \cdot , \cdot ; \phi _ { u } )$ is the kernel of output i, and $\tilde { u } _ { i } ( \pmb { x } _ { u _ { i } } )$ collects the displacements prescribed at the kinematically constrained boundary points $\mathbf { \mathcal { x } } _ { u _ { i } }$ . Setting ${ \pmb x } ^ { * } = { \pmb x } _ { u _ { i } }$ in Eq. (19) recovers $\tilde { u } _ { i }$ regardless of the mean function or kernel, so the reconstructed field satisfies its Dirichlet BCs exactly by construction and no boundary-condition penalty appears in the loss of Eq. (22) [38]. The temperature field $( T = T _ { D }$ on the hot edge), the temperature adjoint $( \mu = 0$ on the hot edge, cf. Eq. (14)), and the design field are parameterized analogously; for the design field, the same conditioning mechanism prescribes fixed phases in non-design regions, which is how the solid-steel gripper jaw (Sec. 3.1) is enforced identically for both design families.

All GPs use the Gaussian kernel

$$
c ( { \pmb x } , { \pmb x } ^ { \prime } ; \phi ) = \exp \big [ - ( { \pmb x } - { \pmb x } ^ { \prime } ) ^ { \top } \mathrm { d i a g } ( \phi ) ( { \pmb x } - { \pmb x } ^ { \prime } ) \big ] ,\tag{20}
$$

with a jitter $\delta = 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ added to the diagonal of each covariance matrix for numerical stability. The kernel hyperparameters $\phi$ are fixed to $1 0 ^ { 1 / 4 }$ rather than trained [11, 38], so the covariance matrix of each output over its conditioning points is Cholesky-factorized once at the start of training and cached; conditioning thereafter costs one cached triangular solve and one kernel–vector product per epoch, and no covariance matrix is re-factorized inside the training loop.

## A.2 PGCAN Mean Functions

The mean function of every GP is a parametric grid convolutional attention network (PGCAN) [41], an encoder–decoder architecture that mitigates the spectral bias of standard multi-layer perceptrons and resolves the sharp gradients and localized features (hinges, phase boundaries) on which TO depends. The encoder embeds the design domain in a trainable feature tensor $\mathbf { F } _ { 0 } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { N _ { \mathrm { r e p } } \times \overset {  } { N } _ { f } \times N _ { x } ^ { e } \times N _ { y } ^ { e } }$ , where $N _ { \mathrm { r e p } }$ grid repetitions with small diagonal ofsets each carry $N _ { f }$ features on an $N _ { x } ^ { e } \times N _ { y } ^ { e }$ vertex grid, and a $2 \times 2$ convolution produces a feature map $\mathbf { F } _ { c }$ of the same size. A query point x is mapped to the local coordinates $\bar { \pmb x } \in [ 0 , 1 ] ^ { 2 }$ of its enclosing cell and cosine-transformed, $\begin{array} { r } { \tilde { \mathbf { \em x } } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } ( 1 - \cos \pi \bar { \mathbfit { x } } ) } \end{array}$ , and its feature vector is bilinearly interpolated from the enclosing vertices of each repetition m,

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { \mathbf { f } _ { m } ( { \pmb x } ) = \left( 1 - \tilde { x } \right) \left( 1 - \tilde { y } \right) \mathbf { f } _ { m } ^ { ( 0 , 0 ) } + \left( 1 - \tilde { x } \right) \tilde { y } \mathbf { f } _ { m } ^ { ( 0 , 1 ) } } \\ & { \qquad + \tilde { x } \left( 1 - \tilde { y } \right) \mathbf { f } _ { m } ^ { ( 1 , 0 ) } + \tilde { x } \tilde { y } \mathbf { f } _ { m } ^ { ( 1 , 1 ) } , } \end{array}\tag{21}
$$

where $\mathbf { f } _ { m } ^ { ( i , j ) } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { N _ { f } }$ are the features of $\mathbf { F } _ { c }$ at the vertices of the enclosing cell, and the repetitions are summed, $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { f } ( \pmb { x } ) = \sum _ { m = 1 } ^ { N _ { \mathrm { r e p } } } \mathbf { f } _ { m } ( \pmb { x } ) } \end{array}$ . Because f(x) depends only on the trainable features in the vicinity of x, training is localized and high-frequency solution content is captured without deepening the network [41]. The decoder splits f(x) into two halves that sequentially modulate the hidden states of a shallow three-layer network through PGCAN’s attention mechanism, which improves gradient propagation during training.

Each field has its own PGCAN instance with separate parameters: the displacement network carries two output heads (the shared mean of the two displacement GPs), the temperature and temperature-adjoint networks carry scalar heads, and the design network carries $n _ { m } + 1$ heads whose outputs pass through a softmax so that the mean function itself yields nonnegative fractions satisfying the partition of unity of Sec. 2. All runs use $N _ { f } = 6 4$ features, an encoder resolution of $( N _ { x } ^ { e } , N _ { y } ^ { e } ) = ( 4 8 , 2 4 ) , N _ { \mathrm { r e p } } = 1 2$ grid repetitions, and decoders with three hidden layers of $N _ { f } / 2 = 3 2$ neurons.

## A.3 Total Training Loss

All parameters $\pmb { \theta } = ( \pmb { \theta } _ { u } , \pmb { \theta } _ { T } , \pmb { \theta } _ { \mu } , \pmb { \theta } _ { \rho } )$ of the PGCAN-parameterized GP priors in Fig. 1 are updated simultaneously by Adam on

\mathcal { L } = \omega _ { c } \mathcal { I } ^ { ( a ) } ( \pmb { \rho } ) + \omega _ { u } \Pi _ { u } + \omega _ { T } \big ( \Pi _ { T } + \Pi _ { \mu } \big ) + \omega _ { m } C _ { M } ^ { 2 } + \omega _ { \mathrm { i f } } \Pi _ { \mathrm { i f } } ( \pmb { \rho } ) .\tag{22}
$$

Here $\Pi _ { u }$ is the mechanical potential of Eq. (4), $\Pi _ { T }$ the conduction functional of Eq. (3), and $\Pi _ { \mu }$ the temperature-adjoint functional whose stationarity condition is $\operatorname { E q . }$ . (14); minimizing them over $( \theta _ { u } , \theta _ { T } , \theta _ { \mu } )$ enforces the primal and adjoint problems in deep-energy form. The adjoint-augmented objective ${ \mathcal { I } } ^ { ( a ) }$ is a functional of the design alone: it is evaluated with frozen states $( \mathbf { u } ^ { * } , T ^ { * } )$ and frozen adjoints $( \lambda , \mu )$ but design-live coeficients, and is assembled from the three terms of Eq. (16) so that ${ \partial \mathcal { T } ^ { ( a ) } } / { \partial \pmb { \rho } } = - \mathrm { d } u _ { \mathrm { o u t } } / \mathrm { d } \pmb { \rho } ;$ its minimization therefore maximizes the stroke through exactly the sensitivities derived in Sec. 2.5. The displacement adjoint λ is obtained from the direct solve of Eq. (13) and enters $\operatorname { E q . }$ (22) as a constant between refreshes. ## A.4 Helmholtz PDE Filter

Let $\tilde { \rho } ( { \pmb x } )$ denote the raw design field produced by the design network and GP conditioning. The physical field $\rho$ that enters all state, adjoint, and penalty evaluations is obtained per phase from the Helmholtz PDE [44]

$$
\left( \mathbf { I } - r ^ { 2 } \Delta \right) \boldsymbol { \rho } ( \boldsymbol { \mathbf { x } } ) = \tilde { \boldsymbol { \rho } } ( \boldsymbol { \mathbf { x } } ) , \qquad \nabla \boldsymbol { \rho } \cdot \boldsymbol { n } = \mathbf { 0 } \mathrm { ~ o n ~ } \partial \Omega ,\tag{23}
$$

with radius $r = 5 \mu \mathrm { m }$ . In the Fourier domain a mode of wavenumber k is attenuated by $1 / ( 1 + r ^ { 2 } | \boldsymbol { k } | ^ { 2 } )$ which suppresses features with wavelengths much shorter than r and imposes a minimum feature size of approximately $2 \sqrt { 3 } r \approx 1 7 \mu \mathrm { m }$ . Equation (23) is discretized on the same unified grid as the physics, $( r ^ { 2 } { \bf K } +$ $\mathbf { M } ) \rho _ { \mathrm { n o d e } } = \mathbf { M } \tilde { \rho } _ { \mathrm { n o d e } }$ with K and M the assembled stifness and consistent mass matrices, and elementcentered densities are mapped to nodes and back by sparse projection matrices: a node-to-element averager $\mathbf { N } _ { 2 E }$ and a row-normalized element-to-node scatter $\mathbf { E } _ { 2 N }$ . The system is solved by Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradients wrapped as a custom diferentiable operation whose backward pass is a second solve with the same (symmetric) operator.

## A.5 Pairwise Interface-Exclusion Penalty

We penalize the adjacency of metallurgically incompatible material pairs (here Ti and Steel), whose direct contact forms brittle Ti–Fe intermetallics [45]. To distinguish sharply between “material present” and “material absent,” each phase field is first passed through the smooth Heaviside indicator

$$
\mathcal { H } _ { \beta } ( \rho _ { i } ) = \frac { 1 } { 1 + \exp \big [ - \beta ( \rho _ { i } - \eta ) \big ] } ,\tag{24}
$$

with presence threshold $\eta = 0 . 3 0$ and sharpness β. Adjacency is detected by the neighbor average $\bar { \mathcal { H } } _ { i } =$ $\mathbf { N } _ { 2 E } \mathbf { E } _ { 2 N } \mathcal { H } _ { \beta } ( \rho _ { i } )$ , which reuses the sparse projections of the Helmholtz filter to average the indicator over the elements incident to each element’s nodes. The penalty is the symmetric, volume-normalized overlap

$$
\Pi _ { \mathrm { i f } } = \frac { 1 } { \left. \Omega \right. } \int _ { \Omega } \left[ \mathcal { H } _ { \beta } ( \rho _ { \mathrm { T i } } ) \bar { \mathcal { H } } _ { \mathrm { S t e e l } } + \mathcal { H } _ { \beta } ( \rho _ { \mathrm { S t e e l } } ) \bar { \mathcal { H } } _ { \mathrm { T i } } \right] \mathrm { d } V ,\tag{25}
$$

evaluated on the filtered field. The symmetry makes the penalty agnostic to which material lies on which side of the interface, and the volume normalization makes its magnitude mesh independent. The continuation schedule reflects a property of Eq. (24) on nearly uniform fields: at intermediate densities the indicator floor is not small, so on a grey design the penalty is not interface-selective but acts as a global suppressor of both phases. The penalty is therefore held of $( \omega _ { \mathrm { i f } } = 0 )$ while the mass constraint is being ramped and the field binarizes; from the end of the mass ramp, $\omega _ { \mathrm { i f } }$ is increased linearly from 0 to $5 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 }$ and the sharpness from $\beta = 4$ to 16 over the following 2000 epochs, after which both are held at their maxima.

## A.6 Mass Constraint and Continuation

With the phase mass densities $\bar { \rho } _ { i }$ of Sec. 2.3, the physical mass of the filtered design is $\begin{array} { r } { m ( \pmb { \rho } ) = \int _ { \Omega } \sum _ { i } \bar { \rho } _ { i } \rho _ { i } ( \pmb { x } ) \mathrm { d } V } \end{array}$ and the budget is $\psi _ { m } M _ { 0 }$ , where $M _ { 0 } = \bar { \rho } _ { \mathrm { C u } } | \Omega |$ is the mass of the domain filled with the densest phase and $\psi _ { m } = 0 . 2 5$ . The loss carries the squared relative residual, $C _ { M } = m ( \pmb { \rho } ) / ( \psi _ { m } M _ { 0 } ) - 1$ in Eq. (22). The target mass fraction is ramped linearly from its value at the first iteration down to $\psi _ { m }$ over the first half of training, with $C _ { M } ^ { 2 }$ penalized two-sidedly so that the realized mass tracks the schedule; after the ramp the constraint becomes one-sided and penalizes only mass above the budget.

The SIMP exponent is continuated $p : 1  3$ over the same ramp, and the energy-interpolation indicator $\gamma$ of Eq. (9) is a normalized tanh-Heaviside of the local solid fraction (sharpness 100, threshold 0.10), held fixed throughout. Each run comprises 10,000 epochs; the displacement adjoint activates after the 2000-epoch primal warm-up of Sec. 2.5, and the best design satisfying the stability and adjoint-health criteria of Sec. 2.5 after the mass ramp is retained.
